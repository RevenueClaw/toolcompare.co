How to Use ChatGPT for Competitor Analysis (Step by Step)

A proper competitor analysis used to mean a week of tab-hopping: pricing pages, G2 reviews, LinkedIn posts, sitemaps, a half-finished spreadsheet, and a slide deck nobody reads. ChatGPT can compress most of that into an afternoon. Not all of it — most of it.

The catch is that ChatGPT is a confident writer, not a researcher. Ask it "what does Notion charge for the Team plan?" and it will answer instantly, in a clean table, possibly with numbers that were true eighteen months ago. Ask it to "analyze the competitive landscape" and you'll get a tidy essay full of phrases like "strong market position" that mean nothing and cost you a client meeting.

So this tutorial is about the workflow that actually works: use ChatGPT for the thinking and structuring, use its browsing and file-reading for the gathering, and verify anything that ends up in a pricing table or a sales deck. I've run this process on SaaS markets, agency niches, and one very strange local services category. Below is the version I keep coming back to.

What ChatGPT is genuinely good at here

Before the steps, it helps to know which parts of competitive research it accelerates and which parts it fumbles.

TaskHow well ChatGPT does itWhy
Summarizing a competitor's homepage and positioningVery wellIt reads the copy and extracts claims fast
Clustering complaints from reviewsVery wellPattern-spotting across messy text is its strength
Building a comparison matrix structureVery wellIt'll give you a sensible row/column layout in seconds
Drafting battle cards and sales talk tracksVery wellWriting task, low factual risk if inputs are right
Finding competitors you've never heard ofMixedBrowsing helps, but it misses small/new players
Current pricing and plan limitsPoor without verificationTraining data goes stale; pages change
Traffic, revenue, headcount estimatesPoorIt will guess and sound certain doing it

Read that table as a division of labor. ChatGPT drafts; you fact-check. Anything that touches money — pricing, funding, headcount — gets a browser tab and a second look.

Before you start: four settings that change the output

Skip this and you'll spend half your time re-explaining your business to the model.

1. Turn on web search. In ChatGPT, the search/browse toggle needs to be active for anything time-sensitive. If your plan includes Deep Research, use it for the heavier multi-source jobs — it runs a longer agentic search and returns a cited report rather than a single-turn answer.

2. Write Custom Instructions. Two boxes, both worth filling. Something like: "I run a [type] business selling to [audience]. When I ask for competitive analysis, give me tables, cite sources, flag anything you're unsure about, and never invent pricing — say 'needs verification' instead." That last clause alone has saved me from more embarrassing errors than any other prompt.

3. Create a Project. Projects keep files and instructions together. Make one called "Competitor Analysis," drop in your own positioning doc, pricing page, ICP notes, and any competitor PDFs or screenshots you've collected. Every chat inside that project starts with that context loaded.

Step 1: Define your competitive set (and don't let ChatGPT do it alone)

The most common failure in AI competitive research is a competitor list that's either too broad or built entirely from the model's memory. ChatGPT knows the famous players in a category. It does not reliably know the 12-person startup that's been quietly winning deals in your segment for a year.

Start with your own list — everyone you've lost a deal to, everyone prospects mention on calls, everyone in the "alternatives to X" search results. Then use ChatGPT to widen it:

Here are the competitors I already track: [list]. My product is [one sentence], sold to [audience], priced at [range]. Using web search, identify 5–8 additional companies that serve the same buyer with a similar solution. For each, give me the company name, URL, one-line description, and why you think they overlap with my list. Exclude anything that's clearly a different category or aimed at enterprise-only buyers.

Then cut the list yourself. A tight set of five competitors you analyze properly beats fifteen you skim. If you sell to SMBs and one "competitor" only sells to Fortune 500 procurement teams, drop it — they're not taking your deals.

Step 2: Build a competitor profile, one company at a time

Here's where people go wrong: they paste five URLs and ask for a comparison. You get five shallow summaries. Run them individually instead, then compare.

Using web search, build a profile of [competitor] based on their website, pricing page, and recent public announcements. Fill in this structure:

>

- What they sell, in their own words (quote the homepage headline verbatim)
- Who they say it's for
- Pricing: plan names, prices, billing periods, what's gated behind each tier. If a price isn't published, say so — do not estimate.
- Top 3 differentiators they emphasize
- Any integrations or partnerships they lead with
- Funding, size, or ownership if publicly disclosed
- Source URLs for each factual claim

>

Flag anything you couldn't confirm from a primary source.

The "quote the headline verbatim" instruction matters more than it looks. Marketing headlines are where positioning lives, and paraphrasing loses the exact language you'll want to position against.

Repeat for each competitor. Yes, it's five prompts instead of one. It's also five times more useful.

Step 3: Tear down pricing without getting burned

Pricing pages are the highest-risk part of this whole exercise, because ChatGPT's training data is full of old plan structures and it doesn't always know which is current. Two rules: use web search, and demand a "last verified" note.

Compare the pricing of these five competitors: [list with URLs]. For each, produce a row with: entry price, mid-tier price, top-tier price, billing options (monthly/annual discount), free tier or trial, seat minimums, and the two or three features most commonly used as upgrade triggers. Include the URL you pulled each price from. If a page didn't load or a price is hidden behind "contact us," mark it UNVERIFIED rather than filling it in.

Then open each URL and check. This takes ten minutes and is the difference between a useful analysis and a deck that gets corrected in front of a prospect.

One thing ChatGPT does well here: spotting packaging patterns. Ask it "across these five pricing pages, what's the most common upgrade trigger, and where does my own pricing sit relative to the group?" You'll often find your whole category gates seats while you gate features, or vice versa — the kind of insight that's obvious in hindsight and invisible when you're staring at one page at a time.

Step 4: Map positioning and messaging

This is the step where ChatGPT earns its subscription. Positioning analysis is text analysis, and text analysis is what it's best at.

Here are the homepage headlines and subheadlines for five competitors: [paste them]. Identify:

>

1. The claim territory each one occupies — what promise are they leading with?
2. Words and phrases that appear across three or more of them (the category clichés).
3. Any claim only one competitor makes (potential white space).
4. Which competitor is positioned most upmarket and which most downmarket, based on language alone.

>

Then tell me which positioning angle is most crowded and which is least occupied.

Paste the actual copy rather than linking. You want it analyzing the words, not a summary of the words. The "category clichés" output is usually the most valuable — it shows you the phrases every buyer has already read fifteen times, which is exactly the list to avoid in your own copy.

Step 5: Mine reviews for complaints you can solve

Public review sites are a goldmine and almost nobody reads them systematically. ChatGPT will cluster hundreds of reviews in a couple of minutes.

Here are 40 reviews of [competitor] from G2/Capterra/Reddit [paste text]. Group the complaints into themes. For each theme, give me: a short label, how many reviews mention it, a representative quote, and whether it's a product limitation or a service/support issue
