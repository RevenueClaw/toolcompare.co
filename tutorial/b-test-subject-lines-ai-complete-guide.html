How to A/B Test Subject Lines with AI: Complete Guide

A client of mine runs a small outdoor gear shop. Last spring she spent three weeks building a "Spring Clearance" campaign — good photography, clean layout, a discount that actually hurt her margins. She wrote the subject line in about forty seconds on her phone: "Spring Clearance Sale."

The email went out to 8,200 subscribers. Open rate: 18.4%. She'd tested subject lines exactly once before, two years earlier, and concluded it wasn't worth the effort.

Here's the thing. When we re-ran that same email to a different segment a month later with a subject line that mentioned the specific product category and a real deadline, the open rate hit 31%. Same list quality. Same offer. Same send time. The only variable was eleven words at the top of the email.

That's the case for subject line testing. And AI has made it dramatically cheaper to do — not because AI writes brilliant subject lines (it usually doesn't, on the first try), but because it generates enough decent options that you stop defaulting to the first thing you typed.

This guide walks through the whole workflow: how to generate variants with AI, how to size the test so the results actually mean something, how to set it up in the major email platforms, and which tools are worth paying for.

Why Most Subject Line Tests Produce Garbage Results

Before the how-to, let's deal with the reason most people try this once and give up. Three problems, and they're all fixable.

Problem 1: The sample is too small. Say your list is 2,000 people and you split it 50/50. You get a 22% open rate on A and a 25% on B. Is B actually better? Probably — but you can't prove it. At that sample size, a three-point gap is well within the range of random noise. You'd need roughly 1,100 recipients per variant to detect a 20%→25% difference with 80% confidence. For a 20%→22% difference, you need about 6,500 per variant. Most small businesses never get there on a single send.

Problem 2: Open rate is a broken metric in 2026. Since Apple introduced Mail Privacy Protection in iOS 15, Apple Mail pre-fetches images and fires the tracking pixel whether or not a human opened the email. Depending on your audience, that can be 30–50% of your list. So "opens" increasingly means "delivered to an Apple device," not "a person read this." Two subject lines that genuinely differ in appeal can show nearly identical open rates because the pixel fires either way.

Problem 3: You're testing too many things at once. Change the subject line, the preheader, the send time, and the from-name in one campaign and you've learned nothing. You've just got a number.

None of these mean subject line testing is pointless. They mean you need to test differently — smaller, more frequent tests with a clear primary metric, and a willingness to judge on clicks or revenue rather than opens alone.

The AI-Assisted Workflow, Step by Step

Step 1: Decide what "better" means before you write anything

Pick one primary metric per test. Not two. One.

  • Open rate — use it when you're testing pure curiosity or tone, and accept that it's noisy. Treat a 2-point gap as inconclusive unless your sample is large.
  • Click rate (clicks ÷ delivered) — the better default. It's harder to fake with a pre-fetched pixel, and it measures whether the subject line set the right expectation for what's inside.
  • Revenue per recipient — for promotional sends where the subject line drives purchase intent. This is the metric that actually matters and the one most people skip.

Write your metric down before generating variants. It stops you from cherry-picking whichever number made your favorite subject line look good.

Step 2: Generate variants with a constrained prompt

The mistake people Make with AI subject lines is asking for "10 catchy subject lines." You get ten variations of the same vague idea, all with an exclamation point.

A good prompt specifies the audience, the offer, the email's actual content, the character limit, and the angles you want covered. Something like:

You're writing subject lines for an email to [segment: past customers who bought hiking boots in the last 12 months]. The email announces a 25% off clearance on trail running shoes, ends Sunday. Here's the first paragraph of the body: [paste it].

>

Write 12 subject lines under 45 characters each. Group them into four angles: curiosity (3), specific benefit (3), urgency/deadline (3), and personalization or question (3). No ALL CAPS. No "FREE" or "SALE" in the first three words. No emoji. Plain language, no marketing clichés.

Why the constraints matter:

  • Character limit. Mobile clients truncate around 30–40 characters depending on the device. If your subject line's payoff is in word nine, most people never see it.
  • Angle grouping. It forces variety instead of ten near-identical lines. It also tells you why a variant won, which is more useful than knowing that it won.
  • Banned patterns. Spam filters and human eyeballs both react badly to the same things. Cutting them at the prompt stage saves editing later.

Most email platforms only let you test two to four variants per campaign. So you need to get from twelve down to three.
