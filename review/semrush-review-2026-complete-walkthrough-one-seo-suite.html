Semrush Review 2026: Complete Walkthrough of the All-in-One SEO Suite

Semrush has been the default "do-everything" SEO platform for years now. Ask ten marketers what they use and six of them will say Semrush, usually with a sigh about the invoice. But a familiar name isn't the same as a good fit, and Semrush has changed a lot since the version most reviews still describe — new AI layers, reshuffled plan limits, and a price tag that has crept upward.

So I spent the last few weeks running real projects through it: a local service business, a content site doing about 40k monthly sessions, and a client account where I needed competitive data more than my own rankings. Here's what actually held up, what annoyed me, and whether you should pay for it in 2026.

The short version

Semrush is the widest single subscription in SEO. Keyword research, technical audits, rank tracking, backlink analysis, competitor intelligence, PPC research, content optimization, and a social media scheduler all live behind one login. If you're juggling three or four tools and stitching their exports together in a spreadsheet, Semrush replaces most of that stack.

The trade-off is depth in specific places and price at every tier. It's not the cheapest way to track rankings, and it's not the deepest backlink index. It is the most complete, and for a small team that matters more than winning any single category.

Who it's for: freelancers and agencies managing multiple clients, in-house marketers who need competitive data, and small business owners who want one dashboard instead of six.

Who it's not for: solo bloggers on a tight budget (start with Mangools or SE Ranking), or pure link-building shops that live inside backlink data (Ahrefs still wins there).

What you actually get: feature walkthrough

Keyword research that goes past volume

The Keyword Magic Tool is still the core of the product, and it's the reason most people sign up. You type a seed keyword, and it returns tens of thousands of variations grouped by topic, with volume, keyword difficulty, cost-per-click, and SERP feature flags. The database sits around 25 billion keywords, which is enough that I rarely hit a dead end even in niche B2B categories.

What I find more useful than the raw list is the filtering. You can slice by keyword difficulty, exclude branded terms, filter for questions, and — the part I use most — filter by SERP features. If I only want keywords where an AI overview or a featured snippet appears, that's two clicks. That single filter has saved me from writing posts that had no realistic chance of earning a click.

The Keyword Strategy Builder takes a domain and clusters keywords into topic groups with suggested page structures. It's genuinely helpful for planning a new site section, though the clusters need editing. It'll happily suggest a 12-page hub when 4 pages would do, and thin content is a real risk if you follow it too literally.

Competitor and market research

This is where Semrush pulls ahead of cheaper tools. Domain Overview gives you any competitor's estimated organic traffic, top keywords, paid keywords, and backlink profile in one screen. Organic Research lets you see which pages drive their traffic and how that's trending quarter over quarter. Traffic Analytics estimates their total visits, traffic sources, and audience overlap with your site — data you can't get from Google Analytics on someone else's domain.

I used the traffic sources breakdown to talk a client out of a bad influencer spend. Their "competitor" was getting 60% of traffic from display ads and social, not search. That's a five-minute check that changes strategy, and it's the kind of thing Semrush does better than almost anyone.

Market Explorer and EyeOn add trend and anomaly detection. EyeOn is genuinely underrated — it emails you when a competitor's traffic spikes or a new page starts ranking. I set it up for three clients and caught a competitor's new product launch page two weeks before the client noticed.

Site Audit and technical SEO

Site Audit crawls your site and checks 140+ issues: broken links, redirect chains, duplicate content, Core Web Vitals, structured data problems, orphan pages, and crawl budget concerns. The report groups issues by severity and tells you how many pages each one affects, which is the difference between a useful audit and a wall of red.

Thematic reports are the feature I'd miss most. Instead of scrolling a flat list, you get sections for crawlability, HTTPS, performance, internal linking, and so on. For a 3,000-page ecommerce site, that structure is the difference between finishing the audit and abandoning it.

Two honest caveats. First, the crawl speed is throttled by plan — the free and low tiers crawl slowly, so a large site takes a while. Second, some checks are noisy. It flagged a batch of "temporary redirects" on a client site that were intentional and fine. You need judgment, not blind compliance.

Position Tracking

Daily rank tracking across desktop and mobile, with location targeting down to the city level. You can track a keyword against a specific SERP feature, Monitor your competitors' positions alongside yours, and set up alerts for big moves. The share-of-voice metric — how much of the total SERP visibility you own versus competitors — is the number I put in client reports, because it's easier to explain than average position.

The limit is the real story here. Pro tracks 500 keywords total across 10 projects. If you have five clients and want 200 keywords each, you've already outgrown Pro. Guru bumps you to 1,500 keywords across 15 projects, which is the realistic starting point for a freelancer with more than two or three clients.

Backlinks

The backlink index is large — Semrush reports it in the trillions — and the Backlink Analytics tool covers referring domains, anchor text distribution, toxicity scores, and new/lost links. The Link Building Tool manages outreach: you build a prospect list, it finds contact emails, and you track the pitch through to a live link.

Compared with Ahrefs, Semrush's backlink data is slightly less fresh and slightly less complete in my testing. On three sites I checked, Ahrefs found 8–15% more referring domains. That's not nothing if link acquisition is your core service. For everyone else, Semrush's backlink data is more than adequate — and you get it bundled with everything else rather than paying separately.

Content and AI tools

The Content Toolkit includes the SEO Writing Assistant, which scores drafts against the top 10 results for your target keyword and flags readability and tone issues. It plugs into Google Docs and WordPress. I find the recommendations occasionally prescriptive — it will nudge you toward a specific word count and keyword density that can push writers into stiff, repetitive prose. Use it as a sanity check, not a style guide.

Semrush Copilot, the AI assistant layered across the platform, is more useful than I expected. You can ask it to summarize a domain's weaknesses or explain why a page lost rankings, and it pulls from your project data rather than generic advice. It's not a replacement for knowing what you're doing, but it cuts the time spent clicking between reports.

ContentShake AI, Semrush's dedicated AI writing tool, is a separate subscription in most cases. If AI drafting is your main need, compare it against dedicated tools before bundling — you may get more for less elsewhere.

Advertising, social, and local

The Advertising Toolkit gives you PPC keyword research, ad copy history, and competitor ad spend estimates. It's a solid add-on if you run paid search alongside SEO — you can see which keywords a competitor bids on and where their ads land. It's not a replacement for Google Ads' own reporting, but it's good intelligence.

The Social Media Toolkit covers posting, scheduling, and basic analytics across platforms. It's fine. It's not Buffer or Later. If social is a serious channel for you, you'll keep your dedicated tool. If you post occasionally and want everything in one place, it does the job.

Local SEO is where the plan structure gets annoying. Listing Management — the tool that syncs your business name, address, and phone number across directories — is an add-on, not included in Pro or Guru. For a local SEO agency, that's an extra line item on top of an already expensive subscription.

Agency features

Guru and above include client management: shared dashboards, white-label reports, and a client portal. The Agency Growth Kit adds lead-gen tools and a listings marketplace. If you bill clients monthly and send reports, the white-label PDF builder alone justifies the jump from Pro — rebuilding those reports manually eats hours every month.

Ahrefs wins on backlink data and has a cleaner interface. Moz is cheaper and friendlier but weaker on competitive intelligence. SE Ranking is the value pick — noticeably less expensive, with solid rank tracking and audits, though its keyword and backlink databases are smaller. Semrush wins because it covers the most ground in one bill.

What I liked

One subscription, most of the stack. Replacing a rank tracker, a crawler, a backlink tool, and a content optimizer with one login saves real money and real context-switching. My spreadsheet exports dropped to near zero.

Competitive intelligence is genuinely actionable. Traffic Analytics and EyeOn surface things you'd never find manually — where a competitor's traffic really comes from, when a new page starts climbing. That's the part I'd pay for on its own.

The Site Audit thematic reports. Structured by problem type instead of dumped in a list. On large sites this is the difference between a fix list and a fix plan.

Reporting that clients understand. Share of voice, visibility trends, and white-label PDFs mean I spend less time explaining and more time recommending.

Semrush Copilot actually uses your data. It reads your project rather than giving generic SEO advice, which makes it useful for triage.

What I didn't like

The price, especially at the entry tier. $139.95 a month for 10 projects and 500 keywords is steep for a solo operator. Comparable rank tracking costs a fraction of that elsewhere.

Plan limits bite quickly. 500 tracked keywords sounds like a lot until you have three clients. The jump to Guru is a 79% price increase, and you're paying mostly for limits, not new tools.

Local listings cost extra. Listing Management being an add-on rather than included is a real gap for local agencies.

Some data needs verification. Keyword volumes are estimates and can be off by wide margins in low-volume niches. Backlink counts lag Ahrefs. I cross-check anything I'm basing a budget on.

The learning curve is real. There are dozens of tools in the sidebar and the naming isn't always obvious. Budget a week before you're productive. The upside is that Semrush's own academy is free and decent.
