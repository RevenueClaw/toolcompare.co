Notion Review 2026: The All-in-One Workspace — Is It Too Much?

Every couple of months, someone in a Slack group I'm in asks the same question: "Should I move my whole business into Notion?"

The answers are always split. Half the channel says yes, they run everything there. The other half says they tried, built an elaborate system over a weekend, and abandoned it six weeks later when the upkeep became a second job.

Both groups are right. That's the thing about Notion. It isn't a tool that fails you — it's a tool that lets you fail yourself, slowly, with beautiful typography.

I've used Notion since the early block-editor days, through the database era, the AI rollout, and now the agent era that arrived with Notion 3.0 in late 2025. I've also watched three different teams at small companies adopt it, love it, and then quietly migrate parts of their work back out. So this review is going to be honest about where it shines and where it gets in your way.

What Notion Actually Is in 2026

Notion started as a note-taking app with a block editor. Then it became a database tool. Then a wiki. Then, over the last two years, it became something closer to a small operating system for a company: docs, databases, project boards, a calendar, an email client, a form builder, a public website builder, and an AI layer sitting on top of all of it.

That's a lot of surface area for one subscription. Whether that's a bargain or a problem depends entirely on how you use it.

The core unit is still the block. Every paragraph, heading, image, table, toggle, and database row is a block you can drag, nest, and rearrange. Pages can contain pages, which can contain databases, which can contain pages. That recursion is what makes Notion powerful and also what makes it easy to build something you can't find your way out of.

Feature Breakdown

The Editor and Page System

The writing experience is genuinely good. Markdown shortcuts work as you type, slash commands pull up any block type, and the drag-and-drop reordering is smoother than most competitors. If you've used Notion before, nothing here will surprise you, and that's fine — it's a mature editor.

What's improved in the last year: Notion 3.0's formula engine (rolled out in 2024, now standard) finally supports proper functions, variables, and multi-line logic. If you bounced off Notion formulas in 2021 because they felt like writing regex in the dark, it's worth another look. Things like date math, conditional rollups, and string manipulation now work the way you'd expect.

Databases: The Real Engine

Databases are why people stay. You get table, board, calendar, timeline, gallery, list, chart, and form views over the same underlying data. Change a property in one view, it updates everywhere. Link two databases together with relations and rollups, and you've got a lightweight CRM, content pipeline, or project tracker without paying for a separate tool.

The honest limits:

  • No true offline mode. Notion has improved caching, but if you're on a plane with no Wi-Fi, expect friction. Pages you've opened recently usually load. Pages you haven't might not.
  • Performance degrades on large databases. Past a few thousand rows with multiple rollups, filtering gets noticeably slower. Notion has made real progress here, but it's still not a database in the Postgres sense.
  • Automations are capable but capped. Database automations (trigger → action) cover the common cases: assign a person when a status changes, send a Slack message when a due date passes. Complex branching logic still means reaching for Zapier or Make.

Notion AI and Agents

This is the biggest change since the last time most people seriously evaluated Notion.

Notion AI now does a few distinct jobs:

  • Writing assistance — drafting, summarizing, translating, changing tone. Competent, not remarkable. Google Docs and every other editor does this too.
  • Q&A over your workspace — ask "what did we decide about the Q3 pricing change?" and it searches your pages for the answer. This is genuinely useful once your workspace has real content in it.
  • Connected search — with Business and Enterprise plans, AI can pull from Slack and Google Drive alongside Notion. Useful if your team is scattered across tools.
  • Agents — the headline feature from Notion 3.0. You describe a task and the agent executes multi-step work: building a database from a prompt, populating it, setting up views, creating a project tracker with linked pages. It can also run on a schedule.

The agents work better than I expected and worse than the demo videos suggest. Ask it to build a content calendar with properties for status, author, and publish date, and it'll do that in about 40 seconds. Ask it to reorganize a messy 200-page wiki and it'll Make confident decisions you'll need to undo.

My rule: let agents build the scaffolding, then take over manually. They're good at the first 70% and unreliable at the last 30%, which is roughly the opposite of how most people want to work.

Important pricing note: AI access is bundled into the Business plan and above. On Free and Plus, you get limited trial credits, then it's an add-on. This is the single biggest factor in whether Notion is cheap or expensive for your team.

Notion Mail, Calendar, Forms, Sites, and Charts

Notion has spent two years expanding sideways, and the results are uneven.

  • Notion Calendar (built from the Cron acquisition) is the strongest of the add-ons. It's fast, Keyboard-driven, and pulls in Google Calendar events. If you live in Notion all day, having your calendar there is convenient.
  • Notion Mail is an AI-forward email client. It's fine. Gmail with a Notion skin and better AI summaries. If your team already lives in Notion, it reduces context switching. If not, there's no compelling reason to move your email.
  • Notion Forms is a clean form builder that writes straight into a database. Genuinely useful for intake: client requests, job applications, bug reports.
  • Notion Sites turns any page into a public website with a custom domain. Good for docs, portfolios, and simple landing pages. Not a replacement for a real site builder.
  • Notion Charts renders database data as bar, line, and donut charts. Basic. Fine for dashboards, not for analysis.

The pattern: everything Notion builds in-house is 80% as good as the dedicated tool it's replacing, and the value comes from it living in the same place as everything else.

Collaboration and Sharing

Real-time editing, comments, mentions, page-level permissions, and guest access all work well. Business plans add private teamspaces so you can wall off HR or finance content. Enterprise adds audit logs, SCIM provisioning, and advanced security controls.

For teams under 50 people, the collaboration features are more than sufficient. Above that, you start wanting the Enterprise tier for governance, and the price jumps accordingly.

Integrations and API

The public API is solid, and the integration directory covers Slack, Google Drive, Figma, GitHub, Jira, and most of what you'd expect. If you need something custom, the API is well-documented and there are mature libraries.

The catch is that Notion's own automations are capped, so anything genuinely complex routes through Zapier, Make, or n8n — which means another subscription and another failure point.
