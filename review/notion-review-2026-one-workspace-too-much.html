Notion has become the poster child for the “all-in-one” productivity space. If you’ve spent any time online in the past few years, you’ve seen its blocky, minimalist pages plastered across productivity Twitter and YouTube thumbnails. But here we are in 2026, and the question isn’t can Notion do everything – it’s should you let it?

I’ve been using Notion on and off since 2019, and I’ve watched it evolve from a quirky note‑taking app into a full‑blown operating system for your work life. In this review, I’ll break down what actually works, what still frustrates, and whether the latest updates Make it a must‑have or a bloated distraction.

Let’s get into it.

What Is Notion? (And Why Everyone’s Talking About It)

At its core, Notion is a document‑centric workspace that lets you build pages out of blocks – text, tables, databases, calendars, embeds, you name it. Unlike traditional tools that separate notes, spreadsheets, wikis, and project boards, Notion smashes them together into one fluid environment.

The idea is simple: stop juggling five different apps. Instead, run your entire workflow inside a single tool.

But “simple” isn’t quite right. Notion has a steep learning curve, and its flexibility can be a double‑edged sword. For every person who builds a beautiful second brain, there’s another who spends three hours formatting a grocery list.

Still, Notion’s user‑base has grown past 50 million (as of early 2026), and its recent AI features have made it even more capable. So let’s dig into the details.

Feature Breakdown – The Good, the Bad, and the Overengineered

1. Pages and Blocks – Infinite Flexibility

Notion’s fundamental unit is the “block.” Every line or element is a separate block you can drag, reorder, or convert into something else. Want to turn a bullet list into a toggle? Click. Want to embed a Google Doc or a Figma prototype inside a paragraph? Done.

This block‑based approach gives you enormous freedom. You can create:

  • A personal wiki with linked databases
  • A project dashboard with kanban boards and timelines
  • A simple to‑do list that grows into a full CRM

But that freedom comes with a cost. Without structure, your workspace can quickly become a chaotic mess. Notion offers templates, but many are either too simple (a blank page) or too complex (full‑blown operating systems from power users). Finding the sweet spot takes trial and error.

Verdict: Powerful, but requires discipline. If you enjoy tinkering, you’ll love it. If you just want to write, it might feel like overkill.

2. Databases – The Real Superpower

Databases are what separate Notion from a simple note‑taker. You can create tables, boards, calendars, lists, and galleries – all from the same underlying data.

For example, you can build a content calendar where each row is a blog post. Add properties like status (draft, published), publish date, and author. Then view it as a calendar, a kanban board, or a table. Change the view without moving the data.

What’s new in 2026: Notion has improved database formulas (they now support more Excel‑like functions), and you can link databases across pages with rollups. This is great for teams – a sales pipeline database can feed into a company dashboard.

What’s still missing: Real‑time collaboration on a single database cell is still clunky. Two people editing the same row can lead to conflicts. And if you have more than a few hundred rows, performance starts to chug.

3. AI Features – Helpful or Hype?

Notion AI launched in 2023 and has matured by 2026. It’s an add‑on that costs $10 per member per month (or $8 annually). You can use it to:

  • Write or rewrite blocks (summarize, expand, change tone)
  • Generate meeting notes from a prompt
  • Create summaries of long pages
  • Answer questions about your workspace (“Find all tasks assigned to Jane that are overdue”)

The writing assistant is decent – it’s not as creative as ChatGPT, but it’s good enough for first drafts. The Q&A feature is surprisingly useful if you’ve organized your workspace well. However, it still hallucinates. I asked it to list my top three priorities from a project page, and it invented a task I never wrote.

My take: If you already rely on Notion for heavy documentation, the AI add‑on can save time. But for casual users, it’s an expensive extra.

4. Project Management – Better, but Not Best

Notion can act as a lightweight project management tool. You can set up boards with status columns, assign tasks, and use timelines (Gantt charts). The timeline view was overhauled in late 2025 and now supports dependencies and critical paths.

But compared to dedicated tools like Asana or Monday.com, Notion’s project management still feels like an afterthought. Notifications are weak – you won’t get pinged when a task is reassigned. And there’s no native time tracking (you have to use a third‑party integration like Toggl).

Who it’s for: Small teams or individuals who want basic project tracking without leaving their notes. Larger teams will miss the automation and reporting that purpose‑built PM tools provide.

5. Collaboration and Permissions

Notion’s sharing model is straightforward: you invite people as members (full access) or guests (limited to specific pages). You can also share pages publicly via a link.

For teams, the big improvement in 2025‑2026 is granular permissions. You can now restrict editing on a per‑block level, lock certain properties in a database, and set page‑level permissions for guests. This makes Notion viable for client‑facing portals and internal wikis with sensitive data.

But real‑time editing still lags behind Google Docs. When two people type on the same block, you’ll see their cursor, but changes don’t sync instantly – there’s a noticeable half‑second delay.

6. Integrations and Extensions

Notion connects to over 500 apps via its API and native integrations. Popular ones include Slack, Google Drive, Jira, GitHub, and Zapier. The API is solid, so you can build custom workflows if you’re technical.

What’s missing: a true offline mode. Notion’s offline support (introduced in 2024) only caches recently viewed pages. If you’re on a plane without Wi‑Fi, you can’t create new pages or edit databases. For a tool that claims to be your workspace, this is a glaring gap.

Pros and Cons of Notion in 2026

Pros

  • Extreme flexibility – you can mold it to almost any workflow.
  • Powerful databases with multiple views and formulas.
  • Generous free plan with unlimited pages.
  • AI features that can speed up writing and search.
  • Rich block types – embeds, code snippets, math equations, etc.

Cons

  • Steep learning curve – new users often get lost.
  • Performance issues with large databases (over 1,000 rows).
  • Weak offline support – you can’t create new content without internet.
  • No native time tracking or advanced project management.
  • Real‑time collaboration still lags behind Google Docs.
  • AI add‑on is pricey for what it delivers.

Who Is Notion Actually For?

Notion works best for people who:

  • Love to customize their tools
  • Need a central hub for notes, tasks, and databases
  • Work solo or in small teams (under 20 people)
  • Value flexibility over polish

It’s a poor fit for:

  • People who just want a simple to‑do list (use Todoist)
  • Large enterprises with strict compliance needs (use Confluence)
  • Teams that need robust project management out of the box (use Asana or ClickUp)
  • Anyone who hates fiddling with formatting

Final Verdict – Is Notion Too Much?

Notion is a fantastic tool, but it’s also a time sink. The same flexibility that lets you build a perfect workspace can also let you waste hours tweaking a dashboard that no one else will touch.

If you’re willing to learn its quirks and enforce some discipline, Notion can replace half a dozen apps. I personally use it for:

  • Personal knowledge management (Zettelkasten‑style notes)
  • Client project tracking (with a simple kanban)
  • Content planning (the calendar view is solid)

But I’ve also abandoned it for:

  • Team wikis (we moved to Confluence for better permissions)
  • Time tracking (I use Toggl Track)
  • Real‑time document collaboration (Google Docs still wins)

My recommendation: Start with the free plan. Build one or two essential pages (a task list, a notes database). Don’t try to automate your entire life. If you find yourself fighting the tool, it’s not the right fit.

Notion is powerful, but it’s not for everyone. In 2026, it’s still the best all‑in‑one workspace for solo creators and small teams who want to build their own system. Just remember: the tool should serve you, not the other way around.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is Notion free forever?

A: Yes, the Free plan is permanent. You get unlimited pages and blocks, but page history is limited to 7 days, and you can only share with 10 guests.

Q: Can Notion replace a project management tool like Asana?

A: For small teams, yes – you can build kanban boards and timelines. But you’ll miss automations, notifications, and reporting. It’s a lightweight alternative, not a full replacement.

Q: Does Notion work offline?

A: Partially. You can view pages you’ve opened while online, but you cannot create new pages or edit databases without internet. This is a significant limitation.

Q: Is Notion good for personal use?

A: Very good – especially for note‑taking, journaling, and habit tracking. The free plan is more than enough for one person.

Q: What’s the difference between Notion and Coda?

A: Notion is more document‑focused; Coda is more spreadsheet‑focused. Coda’s tables are more powerful (like a mix of Excel and databases), but Notion’s block system is easier for writing. Pricing is similar.

Q: Does Notion have a mobile app?

A: Yes, for iOS and Android. It’s usable for quick checks and edits, but not great for heavy writing or database management.

Q: How do I get started with Notion?

A: Go to their website and sign up. Start with a blank page, add some headings and text, then try a database from the templates gallery. Don’t overthink it.
