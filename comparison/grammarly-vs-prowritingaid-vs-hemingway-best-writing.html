Grammarly vs ProWritingAid vs Hemingway: Best Writing Assistant in 2026

Let's be honest: your first draft probably needs help. Whether you're polishing a client email, writing product descriptions, or finishing a novel chapter, grammar checker tools promise to save you from embarrassing mistakes. But with three well-known names dominating the space, how do you pick?

I've spent the last month running all three through the same gauntlet — messy emails, technical blog posts, a few deliberately broken sentences, and one overly flowery essay that should never see the light of day. I wanted to see which tool catches the most errors, annoys me the least, and actually justifies its subscription price.

Here's my honest breakdown of Grammarly vs ProWritingAid vs Hemingway, and which one deserves a permanent spot in your writing workflow.

The Contenders at a Glance

Before we get into the details, here's the short version of what each tool actually is.

Grammarly is the most popular writing assistant on the market, and for good reason. It's an AI-powered grammar checker that lives inside your browser, desktop apps, and even your phone's Keyboard. It's built for busy professionals who need their work to look polished everywhere — emails, Slack messages, Google Docs, you name it. As of 2026, it also includes generative AI features that can rewrite entire sentences or help you brainstorm from a blank page.

ProWritingAid is the favorite of fiction writers and authors, and once you open its desktop app, you'll understand why. Instead of just flagging mistakes line by line, it offers more than 25 detailed reports on everything from sentence length variation to overused words and clichés. It doesn't just tell you something is wrong — it explains why and shows you how to fix patterns across your entire manuscript.

Hemingway (now called Hemingway Editor) takes a completely different approach. It's not a grammar checker at all. Instead, it's a readability and style tool that highlights long, tangled sentences, passive voice, and unnecessary adverbs. The free web version has been around for years, and the desktop app is a one-time purchase. If Grammarly is a sentence-level editor, Hemingway is a paragraph-level trainer that pushes you to write with more punch.

Three tools, three different philosophies. Let's break down how they actually perform.

Grammar & Spelling: Who Catches More?

This is the core job, so let's start here.

Grammarly is genuinely impressive at catching errors in context. It doesn't just flag "their" vs "there" — it understands the intent behind your sentence and flags subtle issues like incorrect verb tenses, misplaced modifiers, and ambiguous pronoun references. In my testing, it caught roughly 95% of the errors I planted across a 500-word sample, including a few I slipped in specifically to trip it up.

It also learns from your corrections. The more you use it, the better it gets at recognizing your personal writing style and recurring mistakes. That's a real advantage if you keep making the same typo or confusing "affect" and "effect" (we all do).

ProWritingAid is close behind on raw accuracy, but it approaches errors differently. It flags many of the same grammar and spelling issues, though its suggestions can sometimes feel less precise. I noticed it occasionally misses contextual errors that Grammarly catches automatically — like a correctly spelled word used in the wrong context.

That said, what ProWritingAid lacks in raw grammar accuracy, it makes up for in depth. Every suggestion comes with a detailed explanation, so you're not just blindly accepting changes. It's a teaching tool disguised as an editor, which is why so many authors swear by it.

Hemingway is the odd one out here, and I want to be clear about this: it is not a grammar checker. It won't catch comma splices, subject-verb agreement issues, or typos. In fact, the free version doesn't even check spelling. If you're looking for a tool to catch mistakes before you hit send, Hemingway alone won't cut it. But if you already have solid grammar and want to tighten up your prose, it does things the other two can't.

Winner: Grammarly for pure grammar accuracy. ProWritingAid comes in second, and Hemingway isn't really competing in this category.

Style, Tone, and Readability: Beyond the Basics

Grammar is table stakes. The real differentiator is how each tool helps you sound like a better writer.

Grammarly added a tone detector a while back, and it's gotten noticeably smarter. It reads your sentence and tells you whether you sound confident, friendly, annoyed, or apologetic. That's incredibly useful when you're writing a difficult email and want to strike the right note. It also offers clarity suggestions, which flag wordy sentences and suggest more direct alternatives.

The premium version includes something called "suggestions for engagement" — it'll tell you when your sentences feel flat or repetitive. It won't turn your writing into literature, but it makes sure your message lands clearly.

ProWritingAid is where things get interesting. Its 25+ reports include:

  • Sentence length variation — flags passages where every sentence is roughly the same length
  • Overused words — catches crutch words like "very," "really," and "just"
  • Cliché and redundancy finder — because we all rely on tired phrases more than we think
  • Pacing analysis — shows you where your writing speeds up or slows down
  • Diction and readability — suggests stronger word choices

For fiction writers, this is gold. It's like having a developmental editor who reads your entire manuscript and shows you patterns you'd never notice on your own. If you've ever read your own writing and thought, "Why does every paragraph sound the same?" — ProWritingAid has the answer.

Hemingway takes a simpler approach. It color-codes your writing:

  • Yellow highlights = hard-to-read sentences (aim to fix these)
  • Red highlights = very hard-to-read sentences (you definitely need to fix these)
  • Green highlights = passive voice
  • Blue highlights = adverbs that can be cut
  • Purple highlights = complex words with simpler alternatives

It also gives you a readability grade level and shows you a word count, character count, and reading time. The genius of Hemingway is its restraint — it doesn't overwhelm you with 25 reports. It shows you what's broken and lets you decide what to fix.

Winner: ProWritingAid for depth. Grammarly is better for everyday communication, but if you're working on anything longer than a page, ProWritingAid's reports are invaluable.

Integrations: Where Do These Tools Live?

A writing assistant is only useful if it's where you actually write. Here's how they compare.

Grammarly is everywhere. It has browser extensions for Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge. It has desktop apps for Windows and Mac. It has a mobile Keyboard for iOS and Android. It integrates directly with Google Docs, Microsoft Word, Outlook, and a long list of other platforms. You can even use it in Slack and Discord through desktop integrations. No matter where you type, Grammarly is probably available.

ProWritingAid offers similar coverage — browser extensions, a desktop app, and integrations with Google Docs, Microsoft Word, and even Scrivener, which is huge for authors. However, the real-time suggestions in Google Docs feel slightly clunkier than Grammarly's. The full experience really shines in the desktop app, where you can open the full suite of reports side by side with your document.

One thing to note: ProWritingAid's Chrome extension works well for basic checking, but you'll want the desktop app to access all 25+ reports. It's not quite a set-and-forget tool.

Hemingway is much more limited. The free web version works in your browser, and the desktop app ($19.99 one-time) lets you work offline — which is a huge plus if you're a writer who likes to escape the internet. But there's no mobile app, no browser extension that works across the web, and no integration with Word or Google Docs. You copy your text in, edit it, and copy it back out. It's a very "hands-on" workflow.

Winner: Grammarly for total coverage. ProWritingAid is great for specific workflows, and Hemingway's simplicity is honestly a feature, not a bug.
