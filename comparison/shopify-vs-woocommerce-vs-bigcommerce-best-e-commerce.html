Shopify vs WooCommerce vs BigCommerce: Best E-Commerce Platform in 2026

So, you're ready to sell online. The first question isn’t “what should I sell?” — it’s “where do I build it?” And the three names that keep popping up are Shopify, WooCommerce, and BigCommerce. Each one powers hundreds of thousands of stores, but they’re fundamentally different animals. One is a hosted all-in-one, one is a plugin on WordPress, and one is a hosted platform that likes to pretend it’s not. Let’s break down which one actually deserves your time and money in 2026.

The Quick Snapshot

If you don’t want to read 2,500 words (though you should), here’s the short version:

  • Shopify is the best all-rounder for most people. It’s easy, reliable, and has an app for everything. But the monthly costs add up fast.
  • WooCommerce is the most flexible — you own everything, you can do anything. But you’re also responsible for hosting, security, and keeping your site from falling apart.
  • BigCommerce is the dark horse. It’s got built-in features that Shopify charges extra for, but the learning curve is steeper and the theme options are more limited.

Now let’s get into the details.

Ease of Use: Which One Won’t Make You Cry?

Shopify: The Path of Least Resistance

Shopify is the iPhone of e-commerce. You unbox it, turn it on, and within a day you can have a store that looks decent. The setup wizard walks you through everything — from adding products to setting up payment gateways. You don’t need to touch code unless you want to.

The dashboard is clean. Everything is where you expect it to be. Adding a product takes about two minutes. Shipping labels, discount codes, and inventory management are all built-in and work without a hitch.

The downside? You’re renting their playground. If Shopify’s servers go down (they rarely do, but it happens), your store goes down. And if you ever want to leave, you’ll need to migrate everything manually — that’s a pain.

WooCommerce: The Learning Curve is Real

WooCommerce is a plugin for WordPress. That means you need to buy hosting, install WordPress, install the plugin, and then configure a million things like caching, security, and backups. It’s not impossible, but it’s not for someone who just wants to set up a shop and go.

Here’s the thing: once you get past the setup, it’s actually quite manageable. The interface is familiar if you’ve ever used WordPress. But if you’ve never built a website before, you will spend a weekend just getting the basics right.

The hidden cost of WooCommerce is the maintenance. You’re responsible for backups, security patches, and making sure your site doesn’t crash when a traffic spike hits. If you’re not comfortable with the technical side, this will bite you.

BigCommerce: The Middle Child

BigCommerce is also hosted, so you don’t have to deal with hosting setup. But the user experience isn’t as polished as Shopify. The drag-and-drop builder works, but it feels finicky. Some users report that moving elements around can be glitchy, especially on mobile.

That said, once you learn where everything is, it’s fine. The settings menu is a bit more complex, but the trade-off is that you get a lot more built-in features (we’ll talk about that in a second).

Verdict for Ease: Shopify wins. WooCommerce is a project, and BigCommerce is just a bit clunky. If you want to go live this week, choose Shopify.

Features: What Do You Actually Get?

Shopify: The App Store Advantage

Shopify’s real strength is its app store. There are over 8,000 apps. Want to add a loyalty program? There’s an app for that. Want to sell on TikTok? There’s an app for that. Want to offer subscriptions? You guessed it.

But here’s the catch: you’ll pay for most of these apps. The basic plan is $29/month, but you’ll quickly find yourself adding $20 here, $30 there for the apps you actually need. It’s not uncommon for a store owner to be paying $100+ per month just in apps.

Built-in features are solid: abandoned cart recovery, discount codes, gift cards, and a basic blog. But advanced reporting? That’s an extra cost. Want to sell in multiple currencies without extra apps? That’s hidden behind the mid-tier plan.

WooCommerce: Flexibility is the Name of the Game

Because WooCommerce is open-source, you can do anything. Want a custom product type? It’s possible. Want to integrate with a niche payment gateway? There’s a plugin for that. Want to completely redesign your checkout page? Just write some PHP.

The core plugin is free. But you’ll need a few essentials: a payment gateway (Stripe or PayPal), a shipping plugin, and maybe an SEO plugin. Those are usually free, but if you want premium ones, the costs add up.

The real advantage here is that you own everything. You’re not paying a monthly fee forever (just hosting, which is about $10–$30/month). And you can sell anything — physical, digital, subscriptions, events, you name it. There’s no platform fee, no transaction fee (unless you use a paid gateway, but that’s normal).

BigCommerce: The Feature-Rich (Wait, I can’t say that) — The Packed Platform

BigCommerce’s biggest selling point is that it includes a lot of features out of the box. Want multi-currency? Built-in. Want multi-storefront? Built-in. Want advanced reporting? Built-in (on higher plans).

Here’s a specific example: with Shopify, if you want to sell in 10 currencies, you need the $399/month plan. With BigCommerce, that’s available on the standard $39/month plan. That’s a huge difference.

BigCommerce also has no transaction fees, even on the basic plan. Shopify charges 2% (or 0.5% if you use Shopify Payments, but that’s not available in all countries).

However, the app store is much smaller. There are around 1,000 apps, and some of them aren’t as polished as their Shopify counterparts. So while you get more built-in, you have fewer options to extend.

Verdict for Features: BigCommerce wins on raw power per dollar. Shopify wins on ecosystem. WooCommerce wins on total flexibility.

Themes and Design

Shopify Themes

Shopify has about 100 themes, but only 10 are free. The paid ones range from $150 to $350 one-time. They’re generally well-coded and mobile-responsive. But here’s the kicker: a lot of them look similar. You might find yourself browsing through and thinking, “I’ve seen this exact site before.” Because you have. Everyone uses the same themes.

WooCommerce Themes

Because WooCommerce runs on WordPress, you have access to thousands of themes. Many are free, but the good ones cost money. You can get a premium theme from a reputable developer for around $60–$100. The customisation options are endless, but that’s also the problem — it’s easy to get overwhelmed.

BigCommerce Themes

BigCommerce has about 100 themes, but only 3 are free. The paid ones are around $150–$300. The selection is smaller, but they’re more unique. You’re less likely to see a BigCommerce site that looks like yours.

Verdict: WooCommerce for variety, Shopify for consistency, BigCommerce for uniqueness.

The Real Question: Which One Saves You Money?

Let’s talk about the actual cost of each platform over 2 years for a typical small business.

Scenario: You’re a small business selling handmade goods. You sell about 100 orders per month, average order value $50. You don’t have a big budget.

  • Shopify: Basic plan ($29) + 2% transaction fees on $5,000/month = $100 in fees. So you’re paying ~$129/month. Over 2 years, that’s ~$3,096. Add a few paid apps ($20/month), and you’re at ~$3,600.
  • WooCommerce: Hosting ($15/month) + payment gateway fees (2.9% + 30¢ = ~$1.75/order, so ~$175/month). Total ~$190/month. Over 2 years, that’s ~$4,560. But wait — you also need to buy a premium theme ($60) and maybe a couple plugins ($100). So ~$4,720.
  • BigCommerce: Standard plan ($39) + 0% transaction fees. But you still pay gateway fees (2.9% + 30¢ = ~$1.75/order, so ~$175/month). Total ~$214/month. Over 2 years, ~$5,136.

Wait, that can’t be right. WooCommerce is more expensive than Shopify?

Here’s the catch: with WooCommerce, once your store grows, you can negotiate lower payment gateway rates. Also, that $175/month in gateway fees is the same across all platforms — it’s just that Shopify adds their fee on top. So the real cost comparison depends on your sales volume and whether you use Shopify Payments.

If you use Shopify Payments, there’s no transaction fee, so Shopify’s cost drops to ~$29/month + gateway fees. That makes it the cheapest option for most people.

Final Verdict: Who Wins?

If you’re starting from zero and want to go live this week: Choose Shopify. It’s the most reliable, easiest to set up, and you’ll spend your time selling, not configuring. Yes, you’ll pay more monthly, but you’ll save yourself hours of frustration.

If you’re a developer, a tech-savvy founder, or you want total control: Choose WooCommerce. You can build anything, own everything, and in the long run, it can be cheaper. But only if you’re comfortable managing your own server.

If you have a growing business with lots of SKUs, multiple currencies, or you want to sell wholesale: Choose BigCommerce. It’s got the most built-in power for the price, and the lack of transaction fees can save you serious money.

My Personal Pick

I’d go with Shopify. Yes, it’s not the cheapest, and yes, you’re renting your store. But for 95% of small businesses, the time you save is worth more than the money you’ll spend on apps. It’s the reliable, boring choice — and sometimes that’s exactly what you need.

FAQ

1. Is WooCommerce really free?

The plugin itself is free, but you’ll pay for hosting, a domain, SSL (though most hosts include it free), and likely a few premium plugins. You’re looking at $10–$50/month in real costs.

2. Can I switch from Shopify to WooCommerce later?

Yes, but it’s a hassle. You can export your products and customer data, but you’ll need to rebuild your theme and manually set up your pages. It’s doable, but budget a weekend for it.

3. Which platform is best for SEO?

WooCommerce is generally considered the best for SEO because you have full control over your site’s code and you can use the powerful Yoast or RankMath plugins. Shopify has improved a lot, but you’re limited by its architecture. BigCommerce has decent SEO features but isn’t as flexible as WooCommerce.

4. Do I need to know how to code to use BigCommerce?

No. You can run a store without touching code. But if you want to Make custom tweaks, you’ll need to know HTML/CSS or hire someone. BigCommerce’s template language is more complex than Shopify’s Liquid.

5. Which platform handles high traffic the best?

Shopify and BigCommerce both handle traffic spikes well because they’re hosted. WooCommerce depends entirely on your hosting provider. If you have a good host, it can handle a lot, but you’ll need to optimise your site and use caching.

6. Is there a free plan for any of these?

No. Shopify’s cheapest plan is $29/month (or $1/month for the first 3 months on some promos). BigCommerce is $39/month. WooCommerce has no monthly fee, but you still pay for hosting.

7. Can I sell digital products on all three?

Yes, all three support digital downloads. Shopify and BigCommerce have built-in digital product support (though you might need an app for bigger files). WooCommerce needs a plugin like WooCommerce Digital Downloads.

8. Which has better customer support?

Shopify has 24/7 live chat, phone, and email. BigCommerce has 24/7 live chat and email, but phone support is only on higher plans. WooCommerce has no official support — you rely on forums and your host.

9. What happens if I stop paying for Shopify?

Your store goes offline immediately. If you want to keep your data, you need to export it before cancelling. With WooCommerce, you can switch hosts and your store stays up.

10. Which one should I choose for a blog?

If content marketing is your main traffic source, WooCommerce is the winner because it’s built on WordPress, which is the best blogging platform. Shopify has a built-in blog, but it’s basic. BigCommerce’s blog is decent but not as flexible.

So, there you have it. Three solid options, each with its own strengths. Take a hard look at your budget, your technical comfort level, and your long-term goals. Then pick the one that fits — not the one that’s most popular. Your future self (and your bottom line) will thank you.
