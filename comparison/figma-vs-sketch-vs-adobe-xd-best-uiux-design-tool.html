Figma vs Sketch vs Adobe XD: Best UI/UX Design Tool in 2026

Three tools have dominated the conversation about interface design for the better part of a decade. In 2026, though, they're not really in the same race anymore — and pretending otherwise wastes your time and your budget.

Figma is the default. Sketch still has a loyal, mostly Mac-based following. Adobe XD is, to put it plainly, a product Adobe stopped investing in. If you're choosing a UI/UX design tool this year, the honest answer is that this is a two-horse race with a legacy option that's hard to recommend for new work.

That said, "Figma wins" isn't useful advice on its own. Sketch does a few things better. Figma's pricing gets expensive fast at scale. And if your team lives inside Adobe's ecosystem, there's a real cost to switching. So let's go through the details — features, money, and the tradeoffs you'll actually feel six months in.

What each tool actually is in 2026

Figma runs in a browser first. There's a desktop app for Mac and Windows, plus mobile apps for viewing and light editing, but the core product is web-based. Since 2024 it's grown well past "design tool" — Figma Slides handles presentations, Figma Sites handles basic web publishing, and Figma Buzz covers marketing assets. One subscription, several products. That bundling is a big part of why it keeps winning.

Sketch is a native Mac application. It pioneered a lot of what the industry now takes for granted — symbols, shared libraries, artboard-based workflows. It added real-time multiplayer editing in 2023, so the "Sketch can't collaborate" criticism is outdated. But it's still Mac-only for actual design work, and that constraint shapes everything about how a team uses it.

Adobe XD was genuinely competitive around 2018–2020. Then Adobe announced it was acquiring Figma, stopped meaningful XD development, and the acquisition fell apart on antitrust grounds in December 2023. Adobe never restarted XD as a priority product. As of 2026, XD still opens and still works, but it hasn't received substantial new features in years. Adobe has been steering users toward Figma or toward Illustrator and Photoshop instead. Buying into XD today means buying into a product with no visible roadmap.

Feature breakdown

Real-time collaboration

This is the category where Figma set the standard and everyone else has been catching up.

Figma's multiplayer is genuinely live — cursors, comments, and edits appear instantly for everyone in the file. You can have five people in a design file and three in a FigJam whiteboard at the same time without anyone stepping on anyone else. For distributed teams, this alone often settles the decision.

Sketch's collaboration works, but it's Mac-only. If your developer or your PM is on Windows, they're either using the web viewer or they're out of the loop. Sketch's web app has improved — you can view, comment, and do limited editing in a browser — but the full design experience is still tied to a Mac.

Adobe XD has co-editing, and it's fine. It's just not a reason to choose XD in 2026.

Design systems and components

All three handle components and reusable styles. The differences are in the details.

Figma's variants let you build one component with dozens of states — hover, disabled, dark mode, sizes — and switch between them from a dropdown. Variables let you define design tokens (colors, spacing, typography) once and apply them across a whole library. For anyone maintaining a design system at scale, this is the most mature implementation of the three.

Sketch's Smart Layout and Symbols are strong and were ahead of their time. Sketch also handles nested symbols well. The catch is that Sketch's library management gets fiddly once you're past a few hundred components, and the ecosystem of third-party system tooling is thinner.

Adobe XD's component states and repeat grid are still nice features. Repeat grid in particular — dragging a spreadsheet of data into a grid and having it populate — is something designers still miss when they move away. But XD's system tooling hasn't kept pace.

Prototyping and interactivity

Figma's prototyping covers the basics well and now handles variables-driven prototypes, so you can build a prototype that responds to a toggle or a text input without leaving the tool. It's not a replacement for code, but for usability testing it's more than enough. Figma also added AI-assisted prototyping — describe the interaction and it drafts the connections for you. Results are hit-or-miss, but it saves time on repetitive flows.

Sketch's prototyping is competent but narrower. You get hotspots, overlays, and transitions. It's fine for click-through demos and thin for anything conditional.

Adobe XD's prototyping was, for a while, the best in the business — auto-animate and voice prototyping were real differentiators. In 2026 they're features frozen in amber. They still work. They haven't grown.

Developer handoff

Figma's Dev Mode gives engineers a dedicated view with measurements, CSS, iOS and Android snippets, and a diff view that shows what changed between versions. It's a separate seat type, priced lower than a full designer seat. Teams that hand off frequently tend to find it pays for itself.

Sketch's handoff runs through its web app and third-party tools like Zeplin. It works, but it's more moving pieces.

Adobe XD's handoff produces specs and assets, and it's serviceable. Adobe's broader Creative Cloud integration is the one genuine advantage — if your developers already use Adobe tooling, there's less friction.

AI features

Figma has shipped the most here: first-draft generation from a prompt, AI-assisted layer renaming, background removal, and content fill in FigJam. Quality varies, and none of it replaces a designer. But it's in the product and it's improving.

Sketch has been more conservative. It's added assistive features, but AI isn't a core part of the pitch. If AI tooling matters to your workflow, Sketch is the weakest of the three.

Adobe has the strongest underlying AI models through Firefly, but those are pointed at Illustrator and Photoshop, not XD. XD itself has essentially no AI story.

Plugins and ecosystem

Figma's plugin and widget community is enormous — accessibility checkers, content generators, handoff tools, icon libraries, design token managers. If you need something, someone has probably built it.

Sketch's plugin ecosystem was once the best in class. It's shrunk considerably as the community moved to Figma. Many popular Sketch plugins haven't been updated in years.

Adobe XD's plugin store is largely dormant.

A few things worth pulling out of that table.

Figma's free tier is the most generous, but the three-file limit bites quickly. Most teams end up on Professional within a month.

Sketch is cheaper at the top end. A ten-person Mac team on Sketch Business pays roughly $2,400/year. The same team on Figma Organization pays around $5,400/year. That's a real difference, and it's the strongest argument Sketch has left.

Adobe XD's pricing is the messiest. You can't buy it standalone in most regions anymore. It's bundled into Creative Cloud, which means paying for a dozen apps you won't use to get one that isn't being developed. For a solo designer already paying for Photoshop and Illustrator, it's effectively free — but that's not a reason to build new projects on it.

Pros and cons

Figma

Pros

  • Browser-based, so it works on any machine — Mac, Windows, Chromebook, Linux
  • Best-in-class real-time collaboration and commenting
  • Variables and variants Make large design systems manageable
  • Dev Mode genuinely reduces back-and-forth with engineers
  • Huge plugin ecosystem and active community
  • Bundles in FigJam, Slides, Sites, and Buzz at no extra cost
  • Free tier is usable for solo work

Cons

  • Gets expensive at team scale — Organization seats are steep
  • Dev Mode is a paid seat type, not included
  • Browser-first means heavy files can lag on weaker machines
  • AI features are inconsistent in quality
  • File organization becomes a chore on large accounts without discipline

Pros
