Norton VPN vs ExpressVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

Comparing Norton VPN and ExpressVPN feels a little unfair at first glance. One is a security suite that happens to include a VPN. The other is a VPN company that has spent a decade doing nothing but VPNs.

Here's what actually held up.

Short answer up front: if you want a VPN and nothing else — for streaming, torrenting, travel, or just staying private on public Wi-Fi — ExpressVPN wins. If you want a cheap bundle that covers antivirus and a VPN for the whole family, Norton is the smarter spend. Details below.

The core difference nobody mentions first

Norton Secure VPN is not really a standalone product anymore. It's a feature inside Norton 360, and that shapes everything about it.

When you buy Norton 360 Deluxe, you get antivirus, a firewall, a password manager, dark web monitoring, parental controls, cloud backup, and a VPN that covers up to 5 devices. The VPN alone, bought separately, is cheaper — but the bundle is where the value sits.

ExpressVPN is the opposite. You pay for a tunnel, and you get an extremely well-built tunnel. Threat Manager blocks trackers and malware domains, and there's a password manager now too, but nobody is buying ExpressVPN for the extras. They're buying the network, the speed, and the logging policy.

That distinction explains almost every trade-off in this review.

Feature breakdown

Server network and locations

ExpressVPN runs servers in 105 countries — roughly 3,000 machines total. Coverage is genuinely global, including less common spots like Albania, Bosnia, and Costa Rica. For anyone who travels or needs a specific country for banking or streaming, this matters more than raw server count.

Norton's network is much smaller: around 30 countries. The major markets are covered (US, UK, Canada, Germany, Japan, Australia, and so on), but if you need a VPN exit in, say, Vietnam or Iceland, Norton won't help. This is the single biggest functional gap between the two.

Protocols and encryption

Both use AES-256 encryption. That's the standard, and neither is cutting corners there.

ExpressVPN's default is Lightway, its in-house protocol. It connects in about two seconds on my machine and reconnects instantly when switching between Wi-Fi and cellular. OpenVPN and IKEv2 are also available if you want them.

Norton uses a WireGuard-based protocol on most platforms, with OpenVPN and IKEv2 as fallbacks. It's fast enough, but the connection handshake is noticeably slower than Lightway — a few seconds versus a couple.

Neither will ruin your day. But the gap widens the further you get from your home country, and ExpressVPN's reconnection behavior is more reliable. On the flaky hotspot, Norton dropped twice in an hour and needed a manual reconnect. ExpressVPN dropped once and recovered on its own.

Streaming

This is where the two products separate sharply.

ExpressVPN unblocked Netflix US, Netflix UK, BBC iPlayer, Disney+, Hulu, Amazon Prime Video, and HBO Max in my tests. It also has MediaStreamer, a Smart DNS feature that lets you use the VPN's streaming benefits on devices that don't support VPN apps — game consoles, older smart TVs, some routers.

Norton is inconsistent.BBC iPlayer failed every time. Disney+ worked occasionally. Norton doesn't advertise streaming unblocking, and that's fair, because it isn't reliable at it.

If streaming is a priority, this section alone decides the comparison.

Privacy and logging

ExpressVPN is based in the British Virgin Islands, outside the 5/9/14 Eyes intelligence alliances. Its no-logs policy has been audited multiple times by independent firms including Cure53 and KPMG, and its TrustedServer setup runs entirely on RAM — servers reboot regularly and nothing persists to disk. The company was acquired by Kape Technologies in 2021, which some privacy-focused users still hold against it, though the audits have continued since.

Norton is a US company (part of Gen Digital, which also owns Avast and LifeLock). The US has no mandatory data retention law for VPNs, but it does have a well-established legal framework for compelling companies to hand over data, and Norton has been subject to that framework for decades. Norton points to an independent audit of its no-logs claims, and its policy states it doesn't track browsing activity. It also collects more account-level data than ExpressVPN — email, device identifiers, and payment details tied to your Norton account.

Both are fine for everyday privacy. ExpressVPN is the better choice if you're thinking about governments rather than advertisers.

Norton technically allows more devices on its top plan, but ExpressVPN covers more kinds of devices. Linux users, router users, and anyone with an Apple TV or Fire Stick will find ExpressVPN the only real option here.

The kill switch gap is worth flagging. A kill switch cuts your internet if the VPN drops, preventing accidental leaks. Norton only offers it on Windows and Android. On macOS and iOS, your traffic can leak during a dropped connection. ExpressVPN has Network Lock everywhere.

Extra features

Norton bundles an ad tracker blocker, a Wi-Fi security scanner that warns you about unsafe networks, a password manager, antivirus, cloud backup, and dark web monitoring (on 360 plans). That's a genuinely useful package for a household that isn't already paying for those things separately.

ExpressVPN offers Threat Manager (blocks trackers and malware domains at the DNS level), a password manager, and MediaStreamer. It's thinner, but each piece works well.
