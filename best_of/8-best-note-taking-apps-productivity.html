8 Best Note Taking Apps for Productivity in 2026

Your notes are the raw material of your work. Meetings, ideas, task lists, research – they all start as scribbles and end up shaping your decisions. Get the note-taking app right, and you free up mental energy for actually getting things done. Pick the wrong one, and you’re fighting the tool instead of your to-do list.

I’ve spent the last two months testing over 30 note-taking tools – from the minimalist (Apple Notes) to the over-engineered (Roam Research). I evaluated each on speed, organisation, collaboration, search, platform support, and, most importantly, whether they actually help you finish things. The eight below are the ones I’d recommend to real people in real jobs.

Here’s my honest ranking of the best note taking apps for productivity in 2026.

1. Notion – The All-in-One Workspace

Notion isn’t just a note app – it’s a database, a project manager, a wiki, and a to-do list all rolled into one. If you like building your own systems, you’ll love it. If you just want to type and go, you might bounce off it.

What it does well: Databases with relational links. You can create a table of meetings, link each to a project, and then view a timeline of all deadlines. The block-based editor is flexible: drag in a toggle, a callout, a code block, or an embedded map. Templates are plentiful and community-made.

Pricing: Free for individuals (1,000 block limit per page – generous). Plus is $10/month, Business $18/month.

Where it stumbles: Speed. The web version can lag with large databases. Offline support is still not great – you need a strong connection to load your workspace. Mobile app feels cluttered.

Best for: Freelancers and small teams who want one tool to rule them all.

Verdict: Powerful but has a learning curve. Not the fastest, but the most versatile.

2. Obsidian – For Knowledge Builders

Obsidian changed how I think about notes. It treats every note as a plain text file (Markdown) that lives on your hard drive. That means zero lock-in, blazing speed, and a local-first philosophy that feels refreshing in 2026.

What it does well: The graph view is the headline – it visualises connections between notes. But the real magic is the plugin ecosystem. Hundreds of community plugins add Kanban boards, daily notes, spaced repetition, even a Pomodoro timer. The search is instant across thousands of files.

Pricing: Free for personal use. Sync ($5/month) and Publish ($10/month) are optional. No premium tiers for features.

Where it stumbles: The default experience is bare. You need to install plugins and configure it to your liking. That’s a barrier for non-technical users. No native collaboration – you have to use Syncthing or a shared folder.

Best for: Researchers, writers, and anyone who values long-term knowledge management over quick capture.

Verdict: The best note app for building a personal Wikipedia. Steep start, but incredible payoff.

3. Evernote – The Old Reluctant Champion

Evernote has been around forever, and it shows. The core features – clipping web pages, OCR search in images, powerful tagging – are still best-in-class. But the app has felt bloated for years, and recent price hikes haven’t won fans.

What it does well: Web clipper is the best in the business. Save an article with one click, and Evernote indexes every word, even inside PDFs and images. The notebook and tag system is straightforward. Search across everything is fast and accurate.

Pricing: Free (60 MB upload/month, limited to 1 device). Personal $10.99/month, Professional $14.99/month. That’s expensive for what you get.

Where it stumbles: The editor hasn’t kept up. It’s still clunky with tables and formatting. Offline sync can be unreliable. Mobile app is slow to load. And the price keeps going up without meaningful new features.

Best for: Heavy web clippers and anyone who needs searchable image-OCR. Also good for enterprise compliance since data is on their servers.

Verdict: Still capable, but hard to recommend when cheaper alternatives exist.

4. Microsoft OneNote – The Free Powerhouse

OneNote is the note app everyone already has but forgets about. It’s included with Office 365 (or free with a Microsoft account) and offers something most note apps don’t: true free-form canvases.

What it does well: You can click anywhere on a page and type. Draw with a stylus. Insert audio recordings that sync to your typed notes. Section groups and tags give you deep organisational control. Collaboration with real-time co-authoring is solid.

Pricing: Free with 5 GB storage. Microsoft 365 Personal ($6.99/month) gets you 1 TB and desktop apps.

Where it stumbles: Search is weaker than Evernote’s. The mobile app is fiddly, especially for editing. No Markdown support. Notebook syncing can be slow across devices.

Best for: Anyone on Windows or Office 365. Also great for students who use a Surface or iPad with a stylus.

Verdict: The best free note app for general use. Not the most elegant, but incredibly capable.

5. Bear – The Minimalist’s Choice

Bear is a Mac and iOS-only app that focuses on beautiful writing with Markdown. It’s fast, clean, and uses a tag-based organisation that feels more natural than folders.

What it does well: The editor is gorgeous – typewriter mode, multiple themes, and full Markdown preview. Tags are nested (like work/meeting-notes) and search is instant. Export options are extensive (PDF, HTML, DOCX, even Jekyll). Notes auto-sync via iCloud.

Pricing: Free (limited to 1 device, no export). Bear Pro $2.99/month or $29.99/year.

Where it stumbles: Only works on Apple devices. No web clipper. No collaboration or sharing beyond simple note links. No tables or databases – it’s purely for writing.

Best for: Writers, bloggers, and anyone deep in the Apple ecosystem who wants a distraction-free experience.

Verdict: The most beautiful note app on Mac. If you don’t need collaboration, it’s a joy.

6. Roam Research – The Outliner for Thought

Roam Research pioneered the “block reference” concept – any bullet point can be linked and transcluded in another note. It’s designed for interconnected thinking rather than linear documents.

What it does well: Daily notes are the default. You write bullet points, link them with [[page]] or tags, and Roam builds a bidirectional graph. The “block embedding” lets you display a piece of a note inside another note. Great for journaling, project tracking, and research.

Pricing: $15/month or $165/year. No free tier beyond a 31-day trial.

Where it stumbles: Steep learning curve. The interface is dense and can feel overwhelming. Mobile app is mediocre. Data is stored in a proprietary format – exporting to plain text requires manual steps. Expensive for a note app.

Best for: Academics, researchers, and power users who want to connect every idea.

Verdict: A niche tool for people who think in networks. Overkill for most.

7. Apple Notes – The Underrated Default

Apple Notes has matured a lot. It’s now a solid, free note app that syncs across iPhone, iPad, and Mac via iCloud. Most people overlook it, but it’s genuinely good for quick capture.

What it does well: Quick Capture from Control Centre, scanning documents (with automatic cropping), sharing notes with others (they can edit), tagging, smart folders, and Pencil support. The inline drawing tools are surprisingly good.

Pricing: Free with every Apple device. No paid tiers.

Where it stumbles: Only works on Apple devices. No web clipper (except through Safari share sheet). Formatting options are basic. No Markdown, no templates, no backlinks. Search is fine but not Evernote-level.

Best for: Casual note takers and anyone fully in the Apple ecosystem. Great for grocery lists, quick ideas, and document scans.

Verdict: Dismiss it at your own risk. For basic needs, it’s fantastic – and free.

8. Google Keep – The Sticky Note on Steroids

Keep is not a long-form note app. It’s for ephemeral stuff: reminders, lists, voice memos, and quick ideas. But it’s so fast and integrated with Google services that it deserves a spot.

What it does well: Voice notes automatically transcribed. Reminders based on time or location (“remind me to buy milk when I’m at the grocery store”). Labels and colour coding. Easy sharing with anyone. Google Docs integration (you can grab a Keep note into a Doc).

Pricing: Free with Google account. No paid tiers.

Where it stumbles: No Markdown, no rich formatting, no organisation beyond labels. Notes are linear and simple – you can’t create nested lists or tables. Search only works on text, not inside images.

Best for: Quick capture, shopping lists, and reminders. Great for busy people who just need to jot things down fast.

Verdict: Not a replacement for a proper note app, but an excellent companion.

Which One Should You Pick?

The best note taking app depends on how you work.

  • Notion if you want to manage projects, notes, and databases in one place – and you don’t mind a bit of setup.
  • Obsidian if you value long-term knowledge over quick capture and don’t need collaboration.
  • Evernote if you clip a ton of web content and need search inside images (but seriously consider Obsidian with plugins as a cheaper alternative).
  • OneNote if you’re on Windows or use a tablet with a pen – it’s free and powerful.
  • Bear if you’re an Apple user who wants a clean writing environment.
  • Roam Research if you want a tool for systems thinking and you’re willing to pay for it.
  • Apple Notes if you just need a simple, free note app that works across your Apple devices.
  • Google Keep for quick capture and reminders alongside another main note app.

Final Verdict

After all the testing, I keep coming back to Obsidian. It’s fast, future-proof (plain text files), and the plugin ecosystem means I can tailor it exactly to my workflow. The free tier offers everything I need, and sync is optional. For most people reading this who want a productivity boost – not just a place to dump notes – Obsidian’s linking and search capabilities will pay off more than any other app.

But if you need collaboration and databases, go Notion. If you need web clipping and OCR, stick with Evernote (or try Obsidian’s plugins first). And if you want something that just works out of the box and is free, OneNote or Apple Notes are hard to beat.

FAQ

Q: What is the best note taking app for free?

A: OneNote and Apple Notes are the most capable free apps. Google Keep is great for quick capture but not long-form notes.

Q: Can I use Markdown in Apple Notes?

A: No, Apple Notes does not support Markdown formatting. Obsidian, Bear, and Notion (partially) do.

Q: Which note app has the best collaboration?

A: Notion and OneNote lead here. Notion allows real-time editing, comments, and shared databases. OneNote shines with Office integration.

Q: Is Obsidian really free forever?

A: Yes. The core app is free. Only Sync and Publish are paid. You can sync via iCloud, Dropbox, or Syncthing for free.

Q: Can I migrate from Evernote to another app?

A: Yes. Most apps offer import tools. Obsidian has a community plugin for Evernote export. Notion also supports direct import.

Q: Which note app works offline the best?

A: Obsidian and Apple Notes shine offline. OneNote works offline but sync can be slow when reconnecting. Notion’s offline mode is still limited.

Q: What is the best note taking app for developers?

A: Obsidian (many developer plugins) or Notion (databases, code blocks). Also consider Logseq if you like Roam-style outlining but prefer open source.

Q: Are there any good open-source alternatives?

A: Logseq and Joplin are excellent open-source note apps. Logseq is similar to Roam but free. Joplin is a lightweight OneNote alternative with Markdown and cloud sync.
